# Learning With AI

A rebuilt static module for first-year medical students learning to use chatbots. All conversations are prepared scripts. Students choose messages, see the reply and receive explicit Correct or Incorrect feedback with a reason. Incorrect answers lead to Try again; correct answers lead to Continue. There is no live model or editable prompt.

## Learning path

1. **Ask.** Define a prompt and reply. State a learning gap, request an output format and specify supplied-source boundaries.
2. **Steer.** Use a follow-up to resolve a term or missing mechanism. Request one question at a time and attempt it before feedback.
3. **Verify.** Separate following instructions from factual support. Check an overstated claim, transform a source note and correct an unsupported addition.
4. **Apply.** Set case rules, request evidence, revise a differential and state uncertainty. The source answer stays concealed until Finish case or explicit reveal.

Each screen contains one decision, with a goal stated before its choices. Renal mechanisms supply concrete learning examples. The separate official USMLE case has three optional hint modes. Notes can be viewed as a flashcard, study guide, concept map or practice question. Starting and closing exercises compare first attempts across five skill domains in the current tab. These are practice decisions, not a validated assessment. Completion time has not been measured.

## Implementation and privacy

`index.html` loads `styles.css`, `scenarios.js` and `app.js`. Only completed step IDs persist under `learning-with-ai-v2`. Exercise answers remain in memory and clear on reload. Reset clears this module's completion flags and current answers. There are no live AI calls, analytics, microphone controls or uploaded course documents. GitHub handles normal static hosting requests.

The retired implementation is preserved under `archive/2026-10-28_171746/` with SHA256SUMS. The earlier voice archive remains untouched. Neither archive is loaded by the application. Local REQUIREMENTS.md tracks cumulative requests and is ignored by Git.

## Educational design evidence

The following full papers, their tables, figures and captions were read before this revision. The implementation screenshots in Prabu Kumar et al., Multimedia Appendix 1, were also inspected. Other supplements concern instruments, search details or development templates that are not implemented here. The quotations below support the stated general design decisions, not exact interface settings or evidence that this module improves learning.

1. **Schlegel E. Designing Online Courses: 12 Tips for Health Professions Educators. MedEdPublish. 2020;9:117.** [Full paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10702633/), Tip 8: “strive for an uncluttered and visually accessible layout.” Supports removing competing navigation, repeated labels and decorative instructional text. This is practical guidance across health professions, not an undergraduate trial.
2. **Fischer K et al. Using cognitive load theory to evaluate and improve preparatory materials and study time for the flipped classroom. BMC Med Educ. 2023;23:345.** [Full paper](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12909-023-04325-x), Table 1, Online layout: “List preparatory resources in logical sequence with clear directions”; RAEs: “Add written answers explanations that appear after students have taken the test.” Supports a clear sequence and feedback after attempts. The noncontrolled first-year course evaluation also reports poorer performance after some readings were shortened (Results, individual sessions); brevity alone is insufficient. Its PREP metrics and study-time thresholds are not adopted.
3. **Carter-Roberts H et al. Usability testing of an e-learning resource designed to improve medical students’ physical activity prescription skills: a qualitative think-aloud study. BMJ Open. 2021;11:e042983.** [Full paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8261869/), Results, engaging design: “Explanatory sentences as to why participants’ answers were correct/incorrect were also favourably received.” Supports explaining consequences after choices. Students also valued progressive explanations and revisiting content. This single-school qualitative study describes preferences, not learning effects. Its progress bars are not copied because the user explicitly rejected them.
4. **Song HS et al. The cognitive impact of interactive design features for learning complex materials in medical education. Comput Educ. 2014;71:198–205.** [Full paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4892375/), Conclusion: “the way in which interactive features shape a learner’s cognitive experience.” The randomized second-year stroke-module study found greater manipulation demands did not improve knowledge and impaired transfer in its setting. Supports scrutinizing unnecessary interaction; it does not establish that all interaction or all navigation is harmful. Fixed conversation choices remain the learning task.
5. **Worm BS, Jensen K. Does peer learning or higher levels of e-learning improve learning abilities? A randomized controlled trial. Med Educ Online. 2013;18:21877.** [Full paper](https://doi.org/10.3402/meo.v18i0.21877), Discussion, Benefits and resources: “It is important to choose the right educational method to the content and purpose of the educational material.” The third-/fourth-year study compared bundled formats; forum access increased satisfaction without a detectable test-score benefit. It does not justify adding forums, animation or social features to this static module. The abstract's inequality signs conflict with the reported tables, so no quantitative effectiveness claim is borrowed.
6. **Taveira-Gomes T et al. What Are We Looking for in Computer-Based Learning Interventions in Medical Education? A Systematic Review. J Med Internet Res. 2016;18:e204.** [Full paper](https://www.jmir.org/2016/8/e204/), Implications, instructional design: “The value of reporting interactive tools, such as quizzes with feedback, would also increase.” Supports making the intended learning interaction and feedback explicit in documentation. The heterogeneous review is not a comparison establishing one best interface. Its analytics and latent-class methods are not implemented.
7. **Gilic F, Dalgarno N, Simpson MTW. Applying constructive alignment and cognitive load in teaching: Case study involving a foundational family medicine medical school course. Can Fam Physician. 2022;68:308–310.** [Full paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9007130/), Using cognitive load theory: “without assumed prior knowledge.” Supports defining prompt, reply and follow-up before branching tasks, then elaborating the same workflow in more complex situations. Its first-term course example mainly reports satisfaction. Its numerical memory limits are not used to set this interface's limits.
8. **Lockyer J, Ward R, Toews J. Twelve tips for effective short course design. Med Teach. 2005;27:392–395.** [Full paper](https://teaching.charlotte.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/885/2023/11/TwelveTipsforEffectiveShortCourseDesign.pdf), Tip 7, p. 393: “set the stage for a subsequent interactive exercise.” Supports retaining brief teaching before practice. This guidance concerns physician continuing education, so its relevance here is indirect; its course lengths and follow-up intervals are not adopted.
9. **Vandewaetere M et al. 4C/ID in medical education: How to design an educational program based on whole-task learning: AMEE Guide No. 93. Med Teach. 2015;37:4–20.** [Full paper](https://cris.maastrichtuniversity.nl/ws/files/73012517/merrienboer_2015_4C_ID_in_medical_education.pdf), Step 2, p. 7: “not only the solution is offered but also the process to come to the solution.” Supports explaining how a useful study message is built before asking students to choose one. The guide concerns a broader blended general-practice curriculum. This module borrows the worked-example principle; it does not claim full implementation or validation of 4C/ID, nor adopt its task taxonomy or development roadmap.
10. **Prabu Kumar A et al. E-Learning Modules Based on Bloom Taxonomy and the Miller Pyramid for First-Year Indian Medical Students: Randomized Controlled Study in Medical Education. JMIR Hum Factors. 2026;13:e84339.** [Full paper](https://humanfactors.jmir.org/2026/1/e84339/), Methods, Validation of Learning Materials: “self-assessment with specific feedback.” Supports retaining practice with explanations linked to the task. The study adds modules to lectures and measures short-term outcomes. Table 2 reports baseline differences despite the prose describing comparability, and appendix labels are inconsistent; neither its effect estimates nor its claim of learner subgroup superiority is used to validate this module.

The practical synthesis is to explain the study workflow before testing it, retain worked messages and useful feedback, and simplify the surrounding interface. The renal source material continues to support physiology only. AACOM and IES support the learner-facing AI/study guidance. No learning efficacy or faculty-review claim is introduced.


Additional verified source: [Google AI for Developers, Prompt design strategies](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/prompting-strategies), “Clear and specific instructions”: “provide it with clear and specific instructions”; “Response format”: “specify the format of the response.” These passages support teaching explicit tasks, constraints and output structure. “Prompt iteration strategies”: “Prompt design can sometimes require a few iterations” supports reviewing and revising instructions after seeing the result. This is developer guidance for Gemini, used alongside the existing AACOM medical-education guidance; it does not establish universal compliance, clinical accuracy or a validated prompting formula. Its temperature settings, agent templates and recommendations about hidden reasoning are not adopted. The annotated renal prompt and follow-up examples are authored teaching illustrations, as requested.

1. **Can I understand the task and tell whether my answer worked?** Checked the prompt/reply definitions, instructions and all 30 lesson-choice outcomes plus 20 starting/closing outcomes. Each answer immediately shows Correct or Incorrect, its own explanation and a gated next action. Feedback receives keyboard focus. Visual inspection confirmed the selected message, feedback and next control remain visible together.
2. **Does the sequence hold together?** Completed the starting exercise, all 15 steps in Ask → Steer → Verify → Apply and the closing comparison. Checked all three hint modes, staged source findings, concealment until Finish case or explicit reveal, all four note formats, both resource-question outcomes and the flashcard reveal. The comparison retains first attempts before retries. Only completed step IDs persist after reload.
3. **Can I navigate and verify the content?** Checked 21 representative views at 375, 768 and 1440 pixels without horizontal overflow, ten public source references, the private lecture attribution, keyboard skip navigation and unknown-route recovery. No JavaScript errors or off-site service requests occurred. No editable prompts, speech controls or progress bars are present. Desktop, correct-answer and mobile incorrect-answer screenshots were inspected.

The case values match the source-based table in the archived implementation. CDC supports the screening limitations and missing diagnostic tests; renal physiology is supported by the specific NIH/OpenStax/course sources above. The prepared conversations illustrate choices and do not establish real-model behavior or learning efficacy.
